## 2. THE PROTOCOL-FRAGMENTATION FINDING — a result in its own right

**Statement of the result:** there is no single "MMFakeBench number" in the published literature. MMFakeBench officially defines exactly two splits — validation 1,000 items, test 10,000 items — with 4-class macro-F1 as its headline metric. The literature nevertheless reports at least **six distinct protocols** under the benchmark's name, plus a seventh axis of variation (answer-extraction mode). Only one of the six is the protocol the LAYA study used. Any ranking, leaderboard, or "state of the art" claim that mixes these protocols is meaningless, and several of the field's most-cited numbers change their meaning entirely once their protocol label is restored.

| Code | Protocol | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Official TEST (10,000 items), 4-class macro-F1 | Benchmark paper; SCPO; **the LAYA study** |
| **B** | Official VAL (1,000 items), 4-class macro-F1 | Benchmark paper |
| **C** | The same 1,000 val items used as the *de facto* eval set by agent papers (300 Real / 300 TVD / 100 VVD / 300 CCD), 4-class macro-F1 | T2Agent; AgentM3D; LRQ-FACT (as reported by T2Agent) |
| **D** | Private re-split: 10,000 train / 1,000 eval drawn from the 11,000-item pool — not the official test set | MM-VeriAgent (and every baseline in its Table 3) |
| **E** | BINARY (real vs fake) F1 — a different, much easier task | MERIT/MIRAGE headline numbers |
| **F** | Stratified 5,000-item subsample (50%) of the test set, binary F1 | MERIT/MIRAGE "test" number |
| axis | **Answer-extraction mode** (Top-1 vs Recall parsing) layered on top of A | SCPO reports both; other papers quote the higher one |

**Non-comparability evidence, per paper:**

- **Benchmark paper (ICLR 2025).** Defines A and B (val 1,000 / test 10,000; §3/§5.1). It *also* publishes a binary table (its Table 6 — e.g., GPT-4V + MMD-Agent binary F1 74.0 on val) whose numbers circulate in later papers as if they were the headline; the same system's headline macro-F1 is 61.6 val / 61.5 test. The fragmentation starts at the source.
- **SCPO (ICLR 2026).** Genuinely Protocol A — but reports every model twice: Top-1 extraction (F1 57.3) and Recall extraction (F1 66.1), Recall running systematically ~7–9 points higher. MM-VeriAgent quotes SCPO's Recall-mode figure. Quote the mode or don't quote the number.
- **DeceptionDecoded (ICLR 2026).** Reports MMFakeBench macro-F1 (58.66 after fine-tuning) without the extracted text stating which split was evaluated — protocol **UNVERIFIED**; the number cannot be placed on axis A until the full text is checked.
- **MM-VeriAgent (arXiv:2609.30698).** States verbatim: *"We use 10,000 samples for training and 1,000 samples for testing"* — a private re-split (Protocol D), with the model trained on 10,000 of the benchmark's own 11,000 items. Forensic note: several baselines reproduced in its Table 3 coincide exactly with official *validation* numbers from the benchmark paper (MMD-Agent GPT-4V F1 61.6 = val, not test 61.5), consistent with its 1,000-item eval being drawn from — or identical to — the official validation split; the paper does not name the eval items' provenance (**UNVERIFIED**). ⚠️ Discrepancy preserved: the impact scout's table labels MM-VeriAgent's 71.5 "(val table)", the MMFakeBench scout establishes Protocol D by the paper's own text; the two labels are not the same claim and are both recorded here (see §5).
- **T2Agent (AAAI 2026).** States it samples **1,000 validation instances** (300/300/100/300) — Protocol C. Its F1 0.759 is nonetheless the figure most often cited as the benchmark's state of the art, i.e., as if it were a test result.
- **AgentM3D (arXiv:2603.02519).** Adopts T2Agent's 1,000-validation-instance protocol explicitly — Protocol C is becoming a convention among agent papers without ever being named as one.
- **LRQ-FACT.** Known on MMFakeBench only second-hand, through T2Agent's Table 1 (Protocol C, GPT-4o: F1 0.716); its own-paper protocol is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **MERIT/MIRAGE (arXiv:2510.17590).** Headline 81.65 is **binary** F1 on validation with live web retrieval (Protocol E); its "test" 81.44 is binary F1 on a stratified 5,000-of-10,000 subsample, seed 42 (Protocol F). The paper also describes the benchmark as having a "train (6,000) / validation (1,000) / test (10,000)" split totalling 17,000 items, which contradicts the benchmark paper's 11,000 items — an internal inconsistency in MERIT's own protocol reporting.
- **"Fact or Fake?" (IEEE Access 2026).** System F1 ≈ 0.81 with split and metric framing **UNVERIFIED** (abstract only) — currently unplaceable on any axis.

**The fragmentation is not confined to MMFakeBench.** The OOC scout found the same disease on the study's other two benchmarks: on NewsCLIPpings, SSDL appears at 71.0 in one published table and 65.6 in another; DT-Transformer at 77.1 vs 65.7 under one name across two tables (**UNVERIFIED** which is canonical); MAD-Sherlock's NewsCLIPpings figure is 90.17 in the arXiv HTML and 90.8 in the PDF (v3) — a version discrepancy between two published forms of the same paper. On VERITE, three different tasks (multiclass / binary True-vs-OOC / binary True-vs-MC, chance 33.3% vs 50%) circulate under the single name "VERITE accuracy," and the 72.7% binary figure is routinely quoted where the multiclass figure (52.1 for the benchmark's own best trained model) belongs.

**Why this is a result and not a footnote:** the LAYA study's headline (0.6020) is one of the very few numbers in this landscape that carries its full protocol identity — official test split, full 10,000 items, single scored pass, 4-class macro-F1, Top-1-style reading. Restoring protocol labels reorders the field: on the one protocol that is fully specified and shared, LAYA stands above every fine-tuned model reported and 1.3 points below a frontier model in an agent scaffold. A short protocol-audit note is a publishable companion to the main paper (see §4).

## 4. IMPACT ASSESSMENT

### 4.1 Sober placement

**A solid workshop paper with one top-decile asset — not a top-decile paper as a whole.** The F1 result alone is incremental against the 2025–26 agentic frontier (whose headline numbers, per §2, mostly live on other protocols). What the study has that the frontier papers do not: pre-registered hypotheses with a single scored test pass — a discipline rarer than the field admits, and one CheckThat!/WOAH reviewers will credit — and a full reproducibility record (executed notebook, 25+ receipts, cost ledger). No competitor reaches its number with a 201M model, 490 training examples, no web retrieval, and no frontier API at inference; that constraint profile, not the raw F1, is the differentiator.

### 4.2 Citation-asset ranking (what will actually get cited)

The field's citation pattern is consistent: benchmarks/datasets get cited by everyone; the first credible method on a new benchmark gets cited; and **warning/measurement papers that change how others evaluate get cited by method papers precisely because they tell them their numbers might be wrong** (the VERITE unimodal-bias precedent). Ranked on that evidence:

1. **The leakage ceiling — 0.9150 (first, by a wide margin).** A source-metadata rule nearly solves MMFakeBench tune. This is a warning about the benchmark itself, in VERITE's lineage — the category of contribution this field demonstrably cites, and one that does not expire when the F1 frontier moves.
2. **The calibration-transfer finding.** Counterintuitive, deployment-relevant, held-out proven (T=8.42 harms: ECE 0.106 → 0.396; matched fit recovers: 0.099 → 0.021), and independently corroborated in three other domains (§3) — a respectable second hook.
3. **The protocol-fragmentation audit (§2).** Six-plus protocols under one benchmark name, documented per paper. A natural companion to asset 1 and citable by every subsequent MMFakeBench user who has to justify a comparison.
4. **The constraint-profile result itself** — 0.6020 test F1 at 201M / 490 examples / no retrieval, with the pre-registration + single-test-pass discipline and the full artifact record. Real, but the asset most exposed to being superseded.

### 4.3 Venue fit

- **Workshops (the natural home):** CheckThat! (CLEF; shared-task + working-notes culture — rewards system papers, error analysis, reproducibility); WOAH (ACL Anthology; explicitly welcomes dataset-bias/limitations and evaluation-methodology papers — the study's second half is squarely in scope); CONSTRAINT (AAAI workshop, Springer CCIS; fake-news detection community).
- **Journals (the realistic ladder):** Multimedia Tools and Applications (IF ~3.0) as the well-worn home for applied multimodal-detection papers; IEEE Access (IF ~3.4, dated figure — treat as approximate) for speed; PLOS ONE / PeerJ Computer Science (soundness-over-novelty review; current IFs **UNVERIFIED**) as a legitimate home for a rigorous conditional result with full artifacts; Expert Systems with Applications (IF 9.4, 2025 JCR) as the reach target only, since it is selective and wants a clear methodological advance.

### 4.4 The split-preprint recommendation

**Split the leakage finding out as its own short benchmark-warning paper/preprint, fast, with the artifact attached.** Rationale: warnings get cited by every subsequent user of the benchmark (the VERITE precedent); the main paper's F1 will be superseded within a year, the warning will not. Concretely, per the impact scout: arXiv the main paper and the warning note together (same week); ship the Hugging Face Space + checkpoints + executed notebook as the artifact bundle; target the warning note at WOAH / CheckThat! and the main paper at the journal ladder (MTAP → ESWA reach). Timing matters: MMFakeBench is ICLR-2025-fresh and its user base is forming now — a warning published while the benchmark is being adopted gets cited by the adopters. The protocol-fragmentation audit (§2) belongs with the warning note or as its second section.

## 5. OPEN VERIFICATION ITEMS — for the final paper pass

Nothing below may be quoted in the final paper until its item is resolved. Order is priority order.

1. **⚠️ THE RLCD-IDENTITY QUESTION (heads this list; the F-obj claim is conditional until resolved).** Two different methods share the acronym: **RLCD = Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation** (Yang et al., ICLR 2024 — PPO alignment on contrastive-prompt preference pairs) and **RLCD = Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions** (a vendor-coined objective attributed to TypeSafe AI's Jev model, Sep 2026 — per the secondary source: "the objective is public, the mechanism is not… no paper, no published reward function… not independently reproduced"). Which one the LAYA library's `rlcd` implements is **UNVERIFIED** from public sources. Given LAYA's typed-decision / TypeSafe-Jev lineage, the calibrated-decisions objective is the more plausible referent — but that is an inference, not a verified fact. Until it is resolved against the library source: the claim "soft_ce beat rlcd in 6/6 matched pairs" may be stated only as a claim about *the library objective as implemented*, and §3's CONTRADICT verdict against Yang et al. applies to the RL-objective claim in general and to Yang et al. specifically only under the first identification. Source for the collision: https://www.sanity.io/glossary/rlcd-reinforcement-learning-for-calibrated-decisions
2. **DeceptionDecoded eval split UNVERIFIED.** Their 0.5866 (Table 6) does not state which MMFakeBench split was evaluated or the extraction protocol. §1 row 2's WIN is conditional on it being the official test. ⚠️ Internal discrepancy in the scout report, preserved not smoothed: the scout's §2 prose says LAYA beats 0.5866 "by +3.5 points" while its own head-to-head table says +1.5; the arithmetic on the sourced numbers (0.6020 − 0.5866) is **+1.5**, which is what §1 quotes — the +3.5 prose figure must not be quoted anywhere.
